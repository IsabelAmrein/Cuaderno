Data strategy: purpose first, then the plan

data-strategydata-drivenfundamentals

This class was hard for me at first: during the first part I wasn't clear on where we were heading. In the end everything clicked, and it left me with an idea that works as well for a single project as for a whole team: before talking about data, you have to know what it's for.

Starting with the destination

Rafael opened the class with a quote from Seneca that I jotted down:

There is no favourable wind for those who don't know where they're going.

Every project, whether for a team or a whole company, starts with an objective. A strategy, he explained, is a purpose plus a plan. The purpose defines what value we want to create and how we'll measure it; the plan turns it into actions. The full path goes through seven stops, and this class focused on the two in the middle: diagnosing where we are and designing how to get there.

Data-driven organizations

A data-driven organization puts data at the centre of its decisions. What I liked most is that the starting point isn't technology but the question: they know how to ask the right business questions so they can answer them with data. These are the traits that come up most, in my own words:

  • Most of their value comes from using data, and they know how to measure it.
  • They ask business questions that data can answer.
  • Their data is ready to use, not trapped in silos.
  • They nurture the data talent they already have inside.
  • They offer customers a different experience: personalized products, prices and communication, and the ability to anticipate.
  • They use agentic AI as an accelerator.

The three zones aren't better or worse, they're stages, and each asks for different things. What was uncomfortable was applying it to myself: I thought of projects of mine that I believed were quite advanced and that may not yet have made the leap they need to move from one zone to the next.

An example: applying for a car loan

To bring it down to earth, Rafael used the journey of a person applying for a loan to buy a car. Since I work in consumer finance, I recognised it right away. There are six moments and each uses different data: setting the strategic objectives, launching a personal loans campaign, receiving the application, deciding risk and price, disbursing the loan and, finally, reporting to the supervisor and measuring results.

The takeaway is that having good data at each step isn't enough. You need a data model that connects all the steps and that every team understands in the same way. That's where the whole class was heading.

From diagnosis to plan: two tools

To move from theory to practice, Rafael introduced two tools from the CDO Club Spain&Latam, created by data leaders from different industries:

  • dataMat, the diagnosis. A survey of 105 questions, five for each of the 21 areas of the data world. Each company answers it honestly and gets a score in each area, to see where it stands.
  • dataToolkit, the card game. One card for each of those 21 areas, which he gave us in class. On each one you place yourself at one of three levels: one point if you're in a bad place, two if you're so-so, and three if you're doing well.

An example with the data dictionary and glossary:

Level 1: serious problem

No common language

There is no shared dictionary and the business glossary gets mixed up with the technical one.

Level 2: making progress

Half a language

First dictionaries exist by area, and terms are added according to how much they're used.

Level 3: well aligned

Shared language

A complete glossary, coordinated by data leadership and kept up by the owner of each term.

I liked thinking of this as a roadmap: the diagnosis tells you where you're failing and the plan orders the small changes to improve step by step.

What I take to my work

Review our own data model. In my area we've built several models that feed analyses, dashboards, predictive models and risk models. The data reaches us from technology and we turn it into business intelligence. I wonder whether our model would meet those 21 areas. It's hard to extrapolate to a whole company, but starting small, one well-built model for one area, matters a lot: it helps tables make sense together and lets AI relate them and build coherent models.

Bring data closer to everyone. Rafael insisted that, in a data-driven company, even people who don't use data every day need to understand what is happening in the organization. For example, a salesperson whose goal is to sell. And it has to be explained in their language: you don't talk about data the same way with technology as with sales, marketing or human resources. Building that data culture, at a level each person can handle, is an essential part of the job.

My project for next year. As a project lead, I want to bring data closer to the people within my reach:

  • For marketing, a data model of their own, with their needs and a vocabulary we all understand.
  • For sales, a small individual dashboard for each agent (not just coordinators, who already work with data), to help them understand their day-to-day and also keep them motivated.

To design it well I need to listen to agents with different profiles, from those who perform best to those who need more support, and understand what they need and what motivates them.

Ecosystem, the keyword

If I take one word from the class, it's ecosystem. The leap to transformation comes when data models relate to each other in the best possible way and create an ecosystem where each area's data feeds the others.

What I take away

Before choosing technology, you have to be clear about what it's for. Then, diagnose honestly where we are and build a small, repeatable plan. And remember that data only creates value if the person using it understands it.
